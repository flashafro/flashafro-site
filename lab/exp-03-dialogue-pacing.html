The Lab

Ethan & Seraphine

Midjourney v6 Runway Gen-3 Character reference Claude (story) Krea AI
Brief

What I was testing

The previous experiments focused on individual characters in isolation. This experiment introduced a new dimension: two characters with a deliberate visual and tonal contrast, designed to exist in the same story world. Could I build a cast with visual dynamics — not just individual consistency, but a coherent relationship between characters?

Ethan: a moody private detective, dark palette, world-weary. Seraphine: a gothic artist, sharp and self-possessed. Visually, they're designed to work as a contrast pair — not opposites exactly, but distinct enough that when they appear in frame together, there's immediate visual tension.

Process

How I approached it

I built each character independently first, using the methodology from Exp-01 and Exp-02. Once both had stable reference sets, I introduced a new challenge: generating them in the same frame.

Getting two consistently-referenced characters to appear together without either drifting was significantly harder than expected. The model consistently wanted to pull both characters toward a shared aesthetic register — darkening Seraphine to match Ethan's palette, or softening Ethan to match Seraphine's precision.

I experimented with different reference weighting strategies — passing both character references at different strengths — and with staging prompts that gave each character specific spatial and emotional positions in the frame.

I also explored motion with Runway Gen-3: taking stills of each character and animating short clips (3–4 seconds) to test how well motion held the identity.

Findings

What actually happened

Two-character consistency is a genuinely different problem from single-character consistency. The main failure mode: the model prioritises overall image coherence (similar lighting, similar colour treatment) over character distinctiveness. You have to actively fight for contrast.

What worked: giving each character a very specific spatial role in the frame from the prompt — not just "Ethan and Seraphine together" but "Seraphine in the foreground left, Ethan in shadow right background, the space between them charged with tension." Frame composition language helped maintain distinctiveness more than appearance descriptions alone.

What didn't work: equal reference weighting for both characters. One character consistently dominated. Better results came from slightly over-weighting the character whose identity was more likely to drift in the specific scene context.

Motion (Runway): short clips held identity reasonably well — better than I expected. The model's sense of a character's physicality and movement style was actually quite consistent once you had strong stills as a base. The failure mode in motion was emotional expression: faces in motion drifted faster than faces in stills. Something to solve in a future experiment.
